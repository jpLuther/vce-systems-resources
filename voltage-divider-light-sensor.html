Try this

1. Use the light sensor preset and raise the light slowly from dark. What happens to the LDR resistance and to Vdiv, and why does the LED switch on?

The LDR's resistance falls as the light gets brighter. With the LDR on top, the bottom resistor then takes a larger share of the 5 V, so Vdiv rises. Once Vdiv passes about 0.7 V, current flows into the base and the transistor switches the LED on.

2. Swap to the dark sensor preset (LDR on the bottom). Explain why the behaviour reverses.

Now the LDR is the bottom resistor, so its share of the 5 V is Vdiv. In bright light its resistance is low, so Vdiv is small and the transistor stays off. In the dark its resistance is high, so it takes most of the 5 V, Vdiv rises, and the LED switches on.

3. Dark sensor: you want the LED to come on when the light drops below about 40 lux. Which fixed resistor works best? Which way does the switching point move when you increase the resistor?

About 22 kΩ gives half brightness at roughly 40 lux. A bigger fixed resistor moves the switching point to darker conditions, because the LDR must reach a higher resistance to take enough of the 5 V. A smaller resistor makes the LED come on sooner, in brighter light.

4. Put a fixed resistor in both positions with the same value. What is Vdiv, and does the light level matter?

Vdiv is half the supply, 2.5 V, and the light level makes no difference because there is no LDR in the circuit. A divider with two equal resistors always gives half the input voltage.

5. Dark sensor with a 100 kΩ fixed resistor: the LED never gets fully bright. Why?

Even in the dark the divider output only reaches about 4.3 V, and the large divider resistances limit the base current. The base current then only allows about half of the maximum collector current, so the transistor never saturates. A smaller fixed resistor supplies more base current.
